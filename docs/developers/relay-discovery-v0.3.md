# Relay Discovery for v0.3

The first v0.3 network primitive separates **where a relay might be** from **what a relay has cryptographically proved about itself**.

## Trust model

A configured URL, invitation, local-network result, or peer-exchange result is a contact hint. A hint is not an authority and does not become trusted because it appeared in a bundled list. A connection must obtain a signed relay descriptor and verify its stable relay infrastructure identity. An invitation may pin the expected relay ID; an unpinned hint learns that identity only after verification.

Every descriptor is short-lived and signed by the relay identity. It binds:

- canonical WebSocket endpoints, or an explicit `outbound-only` reachability state;
- publication, mailbox, query, forwarding, and replica-exchange capabilities;
- supported matching groups;
- configured and currently available storage capacity;
- a monotonic sequence and a validity window of at most 24 hours.

These fields are claims, not measurements. The available-storage value is enforced locally as the remaining allocation for retained publication state, but it is not a filesystem-free-space measurement; it also reflects the current journal admission headroom after any safe compaction. It is rounded down to 64 KiB and re-signed no more often than once per minute, so it does not expose byte-level write activity. Authenticated relay links re-establish on the same cadence to receive refreshed signed peer metadata. Later connection management will combine it with direct observations such as successful handshakes, reachability checks, latency, and fulfilled durability receipts.

## Signed peer exchange

Peer requests use a fresh one-use signing key so discovery does not expose a personal identity or create a stable requester identifier. Requests expire within 60 seconds and cap responses at 32 descriptors.
An empty requested-group list accepts relays from any group; otherwise, every returned relay must advertise at least one requested group.

The responding relay signs the complete response, including the request ID and descriptor set. Each returned descriptor must also carry its own valid relay signature and be active at response time. Response verification rejects duplicate relay identities, stale descriptors, unsorted or non-canonical data, extra fields, responses larger than the request allowed, and discovery frames over 1 MiB.

This exchange provides discovery, not consensus. A malicious relay can still advertise Sybil identities that it controls. Replica placement therefore must use independently observed peers and diversity signals, and must treat signed durability receipts and subsequent repair checks as stronger evidence than a peer recommendation.

## Relay-server integration

When discovery is enabled, a relay exposes its current signed descriptor at `GET /relay-descriptor` and answers `relay_peer_request` WebSocket frames with a signed, bounded `relay_peer_response`. Requests are one-use, short-lived, replay-protected, and rate-limited by transport address.

The relay directory accepts only active, independently verifiable descriptors. A higher signed sequence replaces an older descriptor for the same relay. Same-sequence conflicts and lower sequences are rejected. The directory is bounded, does not evict established entries merely to accept a new identity, and removes expired descriptors. These controls limit memory use and make simple cache-filling attacks less effective; they do not solve Sybil discovery.

The standalone relay enables discovery when `RELAY_DISCOVERY=true` or `RELAY_PUBLIC_ENDPOINTS` contains a comma-separated list of public `ws://` or `wss://` endpoints. `RELAY_SUPPORTED_GROUPS` defaults to `public`. `RELAY_STORAGE_CAPACITY_BYTES` sets the signed capacity ceiling. `RELAY_PUBLICATION_STORAGE_QUOTA_BYTES` sets the retained-publication allocation, defaults to `RELAY_STORAGE_AVAILABLE_BYTES`, and cannot exceed it. `RELAY_REPLICA_STORAGE_PER_RELAY_BYTES` defaults to that quota and caps each inbound relay identity. `RELAY_MAILBOX_STORAGE_QUOTA_BYTES` defaults to 128 MiB and caps pending encrypted envelopes plus acknowledged IDs retained for retry suppression. `RELAY_JOURNAL_STORAGE_QUOTA_BYTES` defaults to `RELAY_STORAGE_AVAILABLE_BYTES` (1 GiB by default); the append-only file may use at most half, leaving the other half for an atomic compaction copy. Existing live publications each reserve 1 KiB of journal headroom for a terminal record; pending envelopes each reserve 2 KiB for an acknowledgement. `RELAY_REPLICA_OFFLINE_REPLACEMENT_MS` sets the selected-target outage grace period, defaults to five minutes, and must be between 100 milliseconds and the platform's maximum timer delay. A 512-byte withdrawal reserve is held inside the retained-publication quota and each per-relay cap. The descriptor's `availableBytes` is a rounded, rate-limited value from the lesser of remaining publication allocation and journal admission space. `RELAY_ADMIN_API_KEY` enables the exact `/stats` endpoint; it stays disabled when no key is configured. A relay with discovery enabled and no public endpoint advertises itself as `outbound-only`.

## Outbound discovery

The outbound discovery client accepts configured, invitation, or local contact hints and creates a fresh request identity for every connection. It verifies the complete signed response, requires an invitation's relay-ID pin to match, and requires the responder's independently signed descriptor to bind the exact endpoint contacted. Only then does the server merge returned descriptors into its bounded directory.

`RELAY_CONTACTS` can provide a comma-separated set of configured contact endpoints to the standalone relay. They are queried in parallel after the relay starts. The contacts remain hints: they do not become authorities, and every descriptor they return must verify independently. Peer-exchange descriptors are cached but are not dialed automatically, preventing an untrusted relay from turning discovery into an unrestricted connection or local-network scanning mechanism.

`RELAY_BOOTSTRAP_CONTACTS` supplies discovery-only endpoints. These contacts are queried for signed descriptors but never enter the authenticated link or placement target set solely because they were bootstrap contacts. `RELAY_LAN_DISCOVERY=true` opts a standalone relay into one-hop IPv4 multicast beacons. It requires `RELAY_HOST=0.0.0.0` and a non-loopback IPv4 interface; the relay adds its local WebSocket endpoints to its signed descriptor. A received beacon provides only its sender address and a bounded port. The receiver connects to that same address and accepts a descriptor only after the existing signature, freshness, and exact-endpoint checks. Beacons do not trigger placement links. LAN mode exposes local IP addresses in the signed descriptor and should be enabled only on networks where that is acceptable.

## Authenticated outbound links

A discovery-enabled relay maintains authenticated WebSocket links to configured `RELAY_CONTACTS`. This lets an `outbound-only` volunteer establish a route through a directly reachable community relay without opening a listening port to the Internet.

A direct relay can also place publication replicas onto volunteers through those already-open inbound sockets. Its operator explicitly lists approved volunteer infrastructure IDs in `RELAY_INBOUND_REPLICA_TARGET_IDS`; an arbitrary authenticated inbound peer is not a placement candidate. The relay verifies the signed descriptor's storage, group, and replica-exchange capabilities, then sends the same signed placement, receipt-inventory, reconciliation, and encrypted-mailbox sync frames through the reverse link. Requests still carry the direct relay's signed identity and the recipient returns target-bound signed responses. A six-peer local integration test approves five outbound-only volunteers, excludes the sixth, obtains five current receipts, repairs a wiped volunteer after reconnection, delivers a publication mailbox notice, and propagates a withdrawal. This proves protocol behavior over loopback sockets, not connectivity through a real NAT or independent operators.

Desktop participation installs the relay's separate entry point as a user-level launchd, systemd, or Task Scheduler job with restart behavior. The Tauri backend may exit with the graphical window while the relay job remains active. The desktop UI saves a bounded list of explicit contact endpoints and starts the service on loopback with those outbound links; the service has its own persistent relay journal and infrastructure identity. Its local administrator key stays in the app data config, with Unix file mode 0600, and is used only by the desktop backend to request exact local status. The packaged relay entry point has been smoke-tested on macOS; service installation in release packages still requires platform testing on Windows and Linux.

The initiator signs a short-lived, one-use link request with its relay infrastructure key and includes its current signed descriptor. The responder verifies both signatures, rejects replays and duplicate connections, applies per-address rate limits and a bounded inbound-link limit, and returns a signed acceptance bound to the request and initiator. Invitation pins and exact endpoint binding still apply on the outbound side. Personal-node identities are never part of the handshake.

Both sides monitor the connection with WebSocket ping/pong frames. Missed heartbeats close the link, and the outbound manager reconnects with exponential backoff. Links also close and reauthenticate when either signed descriptor expires, so a live socket cannot extend stale reachability, group, capability, or capacity claims. `connected_relays` reports the union of authenticated inbound and outbound relay identities.

## Bounded query routing

A v2 search still uses a fresh one-use search key and reaches one entry relay from the client. After checking the entry relay's local index, a relay with fewer than the requested `k` results can ask up to five connected, group-compatible authenticated peers. It uses both outbound links and links opened by `outbound-only` volunteers; unverified peer-exchange hints are never dialed for a search. A receiver may traverse one further link, for at most two relay-link edges from the entry relay. The same signed client search travels along the route, and each hop signs a new target-bound `relay_query_request` with a fresh request ID, remaining-hop count, optional original admission capability, and a short expiry. Responses are signed by the answering relay and bound to the exact request signature, search ID, and relay identities.

Each relay rate-limits forwarded searches by authenticated sender and keeps a bounded one-use search-ID cache. The entry relay waits at most three seconds, merges replies by publication ID, prefers its own verified score for a local result, sorts by similarity, and returns at most `k` results. Duplicate or late replies cannot trigger a second client response. A peer that requires anonymous admission checks the forwarded capability against the original search binding; absent or rejected proof yields no results from that peer. Query routing does not replicate encrypted match notices or make a remote result independently verifiable. Forwarding exposes the search fingerprint to the relays on its path; a signed peer score is an accountable assertion, not a cryptographic proof of similarity, and the bounded fanout cannot guarantee exhaustive discovery in a larger volunteer network.

## Publication mailbox replication

The placement controller exchanges publication-mailbox state only with relays that returned a positive signed receipt for the publication's current owner-signed operation. A `relay_mailbox_sync_request` is signed by that controller, bound to one authenticated target and its exact target-signed receipt, and carries at most eight encrypted envelopes or acknowledgement tombstones. The target checks that it still holds the exact publication operation and that every event belongs to its mailbox. Its signed response returns one bounded page of its own mailbox state. Each relay fsyncs a newly learned event before reporting success; a rejected or lost exchange leaves the page queued for retry. Cursors cycle through retained state, so reconnects and lost replica journals are repaired without a full mailbox listing in one frame.

Acknowledgements are observed-remove tombstones: an acknowledgement learned before its envelope still prevents that envelope from appearing later. Tombstones remain charged to the mailbox and journal quotas until expiry. This exchange is eventually consistent and does not provide an immediate mailbox durability quorum; clients should not infer that a deposit has already reached three relays from a single relay acknowledgement. The ciphertext stays opaque to relays. A dishonest selected relay can still inject bogus encrypted events within its quotas; clients validate decrypted signatures and message bindings. Pairwise relationship mailboxes are separate from publication placement and are not covered by this exchange.

Independent relays may sign different match operations for the same publication pair. New match notices derive the recipient envelope ID from the pair's stable match ID and mailbox ID, so a mailbox retains at most one notice for that logical match. The personal node's encrypted match table also has one row per match and recipient publication, including after restart. If relays gave that shared envelope ID different expiries, an acknowledgement removes either variant and the replicated tombstone keeps the later observed expiry. An unseen copy on a long-offline relay could reappear after a shorter tombstone expires; the personal node's durable match key still prevents a second visible notification. Older operation-specific envelope IDs remain readable during migration and use that same personal-node deduplication.

The [five-relay churn harness](churn-harness-v0.3.md) uses five outbound-only volunteers and a directly reachable controller. It isolates one live volunteer's controller link while leaving its client mailbox available, abruptly stops a second volunteer, and then restarts that volunteer after erasing its operation journal. It checks that three connected replicas remain, the lost publication is repaired, and an acknowledgement made on the isolated relay eventually clears all five copies after the link heals. It also stops and restarts the controller, checking that a volunteer can search its retained copy while the controller is offline. The harness emits timing and receipt measurements for this controlled failure schedule; it does not measure availability across real volunteer networks or prove independent relay ownership.

The harness also publishes a distinct record while the first relay is isolated, verifies that a search through that relay misses it, then measures repair to five receipts and search after reconnection. One local run took about 12.5 seconds from link healing to five receipts and about 1.9 seconds for the healed search. These timings are test-machine observations, not availability targets; the test still needs broader outage schedules and real-network measurement.

Desktop owner controls apply to discretionary new work. A publication storage quota is enforced by the retained-state ledger. A per-hour new-work ingress budget, a per-hour measured total-traffic budget, a process CPU budget per minute, local active-hours window, and external-power requirement can decline new publications, first-time replica admissions, and searches. The total-traffic budget counts HTTP and WebSocket ingress and egress, including outbound relay and discovery connections, plus LAN-discovery UDP payload bytes. CPU covers the standalone relay process. Already held records continue to receive repairs, mailbox acknowledgements, and withdrawals, so bandwidth and CPU budgets are admission thresholds rather than hard caps. The authenticated desktop status shows lifetime aggregate transport and LAN ingress and egress, process CPU, data-file, mailbox, and journal bytes. Resource totals are checkpointed every 30 seconds, on each accepted admission when a resource policy is configured, and on clean shutdown; a crash can lose the traffic and CPU measured since the last checkpoint. The hourly and minute budget windows are persisted on admission and retained across restarts. Publication and journal commitment floors include retained state and reserves for accepted withdrawals and acknowledgements. A relay refuses a lower configured storage quota on restart, and the desktop checks its last observed floor before reinstalling the background service. The receipt-minimum count records durable writes for current operations, not proof that those volunteers are online now.

## Replica placement and receipts

An authenticated link can carry `relay_replica_put` requests for owner-signed publication records and tombstones. The sending relay signs the complete request with its infrastructure identity, including a one-use request ID and short validity window. The receiver requires that identity to match the authenticated link, verifies the nested publication-owner signature, applies a separate per-relay rate limit, and enforces its advertised matching groups. It keeps a bounded global and per-sender replay cache after those checks. A duplicate of a cached exact signed request receives the same signed receipt, which makes a lost response safe to retry; a request-ID collision with a different signature closes the link.

An accepted operation passes through the same append-only journal and materialized publication store as a direct client submission. The receiver fsyncs a new journal record before returning a `relay_replica_receipt`. The receipt is signed by the receiving relay and binds the sender, request, publication ID, operation kind, sequence, and owner signature. Exact retries receive an `already-stored` receipt; stale, conflicting, terminal, expired, unsupported, or failed writes receive a signed rejection and do not count as durable copies.

The placement controller journals an intent before a locally submitted publication operation is committed. An intent records the exact owner-signed operation, an ordered set of relay IDs, a desired count (five by default), and a receipt-confirmed quorum (three by default). It journals each positive receipt before counting it. On restart, the relay first rebuilds publication state, then replays matching intents and receipts; an intent without its publication operation is ignored.

Only live, direct, authenticated links that were explicitly configured or invited are eligible in this first version. The controller checks the relay's advertised publication-storage, replica-exchange, group, and nonzero-capacity claims, then chooses a stable subset using the publication ID. It starts with fewer targets when fewer configured volunteers are online, grows toward five when they reconnect, retries pending targets on reconnect and at a bounded interval, and preserves previous targets when publishing an update or tombstone. A disconnected selected target remains in the intent during the outage grace period. After that period, the controller replaces it only when a connected eligible spare can keep the intent at least as large; it journals the new intent before copying the operation and does not add the old relay to the permanent-rejection set. A controller restart begins a fresh grace period. Peer-exchange descriptors are still never auto-dialed.

Before filling a placement from the stable score order, the controller prefers one relay from each observed network failure domain. The evidence comes from the endpoint that completed the authenticated handshake: IPv4 addresses are grouped by `/24`, IPv6 addresses by `/48`, and DNS names by exact normalized hostname. A second pass fills any remaining positions from already represented or unknown domains, so a small volunteer network can still reach its replica target. Multiple endpoints authenticated as the same relay identity count once. This is a failure-correlation heuristic only. Distinct domains do not prove distinct operators, autonomous systems, hosting providers, or physical locations, and DNS aliases can hide shared infrastructure.

`RelayServer.getReplicaPlacementStatus(publicationId)` returns the current intent, selected targets, receipt-confirmed targets, pending targets, recent inventory-present and inventory-missing targets, quorum state, and any `reconciliationRequiredRelayIds`. A reconciliation requirement pauses automatic retries and expansion for that exact operation, so receipt counts do not claim a healthy placement while the source may be behind. The authenticated `/stats` endpoint exposes `durability_receipts`, `placement_intents`, and `minimum_confirmed_placements`. A receipt is evidence of a past fsync, not proof that a relay is currently reachable or independently operated. The status deliberately calls this **receipt-confirmed**, rather than healthy.

## Receipt-authorized inventory batches

After a relay has positive durability receipts for a target, it can send a signed `relay_replica_inventory_batch_request` over the existing authenticated link. The request carries a canonical, duplicate-free list of up to 64 target-signed receipts and a fresh, short-lived request ID. Each receipt authorizes a check for one exact operation: a link peer cannot ask whether arbitrary publication IDs are present or enumerate the target's full publication set. The original single-receipt request remains protocol-compatible, but the repair controller uses batches.

The target validates the requester, its own relay ID, every embedded durable receipt, link capability, freshness, rate limit, and replay state. The rate limiter charges the whole batch atomically by receipt count. It replies with one signed `relay_replica_inventory_batch_response`, bound to the request signature and ordered receipt signatures, containing a compact presence bitmap or a rate-limit rejection. A set bit means the target's current operation exactly matches; a different revision, tombstone, expired record, conflict, or absent record is represented only by a clear bit, so the target does not disclose what it stores instead. This reduces response and signature overhead, while the request size still grows with the number of receipts.

Inventory answers are deliberately memory-only. A new write receipt clears an older observation, and a relay restart treats targets as unchecked and asks again on the next repair pass. A missing bit puts that target back into the repair set even though its old receipt remains durable historical evidence. If a target already holds a newer owner-signed operation, the normal replica-put rules reject the older repair request; inventory checks never permit rollback.

Repair resumes on the same target when it reconnects under the relay identity named in its receipt. If it stays offline beyond the grace period, a live spare can instead receive the exact operation and provide a fresh receipt. A volunteer that loses both its stored data and its relay identity is a new target and can be selected as a spare after authenticating normally. Existing selected targets are attempted before the controller expands an underfull placement, so a signed divergent response in that repair pass fences the old operation before a new volunteer receives it. A signed `capacity-exhausted` response removes a target and selects a replacement after the receiver evaluates the exact owner-signed operation, so it cannot conceal a newer state. A signed `stale`, `conflict`, or `terminal` response is persisted as a target-specific capability for a read-only `relay_replica_reconciliation_request`; the target can return only its current owner-signed operation for that same publication or a narrow unavailable result. If the answer is an unambiguous valid successor, the source adopts it locally and retires the old placement intent without creating a new outbound intent. Same-sequence live conflicts, missing state, `unsupported-group`, and `invalid` remain quarantined. The target never replays the old placement request during reconciliation, so losing a newer state cannot resurrect it. `rate-limited` and `persistence-failed` responses retain the target for bounded retry; expired live publications are not repaired.

Before normal shutdown, a relay groups the exact current operations it hosts by their durably recorded original controller. For each controller that still has an authenticated inbound link, it sends canonical signed `relay_replica_handoff_request` batches of at most 64 operation references. The controller accepts an entry only when its current placement generation matches and it holds the retiring target's durability receipt for the same operation signature. It fsyncs a new intent that permanently excludes that target for this operation, selects a connected eligible replacement where possible, queues repair, and returns a signed response with accepted and already-safe-elsewhere bitmaps. The retirement request cannot enumerate controller data or cause arbitrary placement changes. The relay waits up to two seconds per reachable controller before closing links. An acknowledgement proves the replacement decision was persisted; it does not prove that the replacement write completed. A clear safe-elsewhere bit means the minimum receipt-confirmed count was not present without the retiring relay at acknowledgement time. Abrupt termination, unavailable controllers, and unacknowledged entries use the outage grace and spare-replacement path instead.

Storage capacity remains a self-reported claim to other relays, but the receiver enforces its own retained-publication and journal allocations before its fsynced journal write. A replica that will not fit receives a signed `capacity-exhausted` receipt and no journal record. A live owner-signed publication reserves its serialized state plus 512 bytes for its own terminal operation in the retained-state quota, and 1 KiB in the journal budget. Separately, the retained-state quota holds one bounded 512-byte pool for a valid first-seen tombstone; the journal reserves 1 KiB for one such terminal write until one arrives. These reserves cannot guarantee unlimited first-seen tombstones on finite volunteer storage. New rows carry durable `local`, `replica`, or `legacy` provenance. A local tag keeps the relay's own retained state outside inbound-peer accounting even if recovery recreates the infrastructure identity; a replica tag preserves the authenticated first sender through later updates and tombstones. Historical unmarked rows are never guessed from the owner signature or timing: they use one internal legacy allocation bucket, which is subject to the per-relay cap but excluded from the named `replica_storage_relays` count. A new update or tombstone of that historical state is written with the explicit legacy tag, so compaction retains the classification. Exact local or authorized statistics report its bytes as `legacy_unattributed_storage_reserved_bytes`; the relay logs a startup warning while it is nonzero. Natural expiry removes a record from matching but does not release retained-state allocation; it may still be renewed or tombstoned. Mailbox envelopes must expire within 30 days of creation. An acknowledged envelope cannot be redeposited before expiry; its ID remains charged to the mailbox quota. A fsynced replacement journal compacts superseded publication revisions except exact revisions needed by retained signed matches, plus expired mailbox history. A tombstone keeps the last live record needed for replay and accounting. Expired match generations are removed; the current match decision becomes a signed checkpoint without encrypted notices after their delivery lifetime ends while both publications are active. The latest placement intent and its selected targets' positive receipts survive; older revisions and deselected receipts do not. A resolved reconciliation adoption becomes the adopted owner-signed publication operation with its original allocation principal. The byte quotas do not cover relay identity files or unrelated filesystem use. A public descriptor is a coarse placement hint; signed receipt responses are authoritative. Operators must not use the five-target count as a Sybil-resistance or availability guarantee.

## Next integration step

Pairwise relationship mailboxes now use a separate policy: a relay exchanges the original relationship-signed deposits and acknowledgements with up to its configured target count of connected mailbox-capable volunteers, preferring distinct observed network domains. A target fsyncs a newly accepted operation before sending its signed sync response. Reconnects and empty-journal restarts repair from retained signed operations; clients merge mailbox reads across explicitly configured fallback URLs. This is best-effort placement without durable relationship-mailbox receipts or automatic discovery of a dead original relay's targets. Next work adds broader CPU and bandwidth limits, stronger diversity evidence, and measured repair behavior under churn. The compactor preserves the last live predecessor of a tombstone and the first accepted allocation principal, including conservative legacy classification. Active placements, signed match decisions, unexpired delivery history, and tombstones still consume finite journal space. The hard byte budget may halt new writes when those obligations cannot be safely removed. Lowering a quota below retained commitments now prevents startup until the operator raises it or safe compaction frees space; deleting the journal would break outstanding durability promises.
