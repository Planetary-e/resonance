# v0.4 private transport: implementation sequence

The v0.3 protocol protects some payloads, but a relay that receives a request can still associate its network source with the operation. v0.4 must separate those observations for publish, search, and mailbox traffic. The [roadmap](../../ROADMAP.md) defines the full completion test; a TLS connection alone does not satisfy it.

## Threat model and route

For each operation the client selects two **distinct, verified** volunteer relays: an entry and a destination. The client encrypts the operation for the destination and wraps it in an independently encrypted instruction for the entry. The entry sees the client's address and the next hop, but cannot read the operation. The destination sees the operation and the entry's address, but should not receive the client's address or stable client identifier. Replies follow a short-lived return route. Selection must account for distinct relay identities and observed failure domains; bootstrap hints alone are not trust evidence.

This construction assumes the two relays do not collude. Colluding relays can link the forwarded ciphertext and request ID. Local client batching and jitter, plus shared request and reply queues at the entry, can shuffle traffic within busy collection windows. They do not create other users during quiet periods. Route rotation and adversarial measurements across independent network paths remain open. A volunteer-only network cannot promise an available two-hop route at every moment. A client should queue or retry a private operation when it cannot find two suitable relays, rather than silently fall back to a direct request.

Use [HPKE (RFC 9180)](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9180) for hop payloads, with authenticated relay public keys. The envelope implementation uses the [@hpke/core](https://github.com/dajiaji/hpke-js/tree/main/packages/core) P-256/HKDF-SHA256/AES-128-GCM suite. Each relay signs a separate encryption-key attestation with its infrastructure identity; discovery includes the current attestation and the receiver checks that it matches the contacted relay. Attestations last at most 15 minutes; requests last at most 30 seconds. The entry and destination layers use different HPKE contexts and bind the stage, recipient, key, request ID, and expiry as authenticated metadata. A fresh client-held response key is sealed inside the destination layer, so the destination can encrypt its signed reply without revealing reply contents to the entry. Operations and signed replies are limited to 190 KiB; nested base64 and padding need this lower limit to keep the outer frame below 512 KiB. Each hop fsyncs bounded, short-lived replay evidence before forwarding or applying a request. It restores unexpired evidence before listening after restart and refuses work if the replay log is corrupt or full. The relay rotates its encryption key and retains older active keys for in-flight requests. Do not reuse the v0.3 DID or an admission credential in an observable outer frame.

## Delivery slices

1. **Transport boundary (started):** Reject Internet-facing `ws://` URLs before client, discovery, and relay-link dials, and before relay advertisement. Permit cleartext loopback and private-LAN endpoints for local testing. This does not yet provide TLS service or remove local-network observers.
2. **Reachable TLS (started):** The standalone relay can load a certificate and private key with `RELAY_TLS_CERT_FILE` and `RELAY_TLS_KEY_FILE` to serve HTTPS/WSS directly. Set `RELAY_PUBLIC_ENDPOINTS=wss://your-host:port/` with a certificate whose name matches that host. Both files must be supplied, and a TLS listener cannot advertise `ws://`. A direct listener rejects an expired certificate or one that does not cover every advertised hostname. Alternatively, terminate TLS in a trusted local reverse proxy and bind the relay's cleartext backend to `RELAY_HOST=127.0.0.1`; the server rejects a non-loopback backend when advertising `wss://` without its own TLS configuration. A cleartext listener drops inbound connections from outside loopback and private-LAN address ranges. Keep the backend port inaccessible from outside the machine. Certificate issuance, renewal, and cross-household validation remain operator/deployment work. Audit inbound paths, reverse proxies, and bootstrap/configuration surfaces.

   A direct TLS relay can be started with:

   ```sh
   RELAY_HOST=0.0.0.0 RELAY_PORT=9090 \
   RELAY_TLS_CERT_FILE=/secure/path/fullchain.pem \
   RELAY_TLS_KEY_FILE=/secure/path/privkey.pem \
   RELAY_IDENTITY_PASSPHRASE='your-long-secret-passphrase' \
   RELAY_PUBLIC_ENDPOINTS=wss://relay.example.org:9090/ \
   npm run start --workspace=@resonance/relay
   ```

   The relay binds to `127.0.0.1` by default. Use `RELAY_HOST=0.0.0.0` only when you intend to accept external connections through a TLS listener, or when explicitly opting into private-LAN discovery. A reachable WSS relay requires `RELAY_IDENTITY_PASSPHRASE` of at least 16 characters. Its infrastructure identity is encrypted with scrypt and AES-GCM; setting the passphrase on an existing installation atomically migrates its plaintext identity without changing the relay ID. Keep the passphrase outside source control and retain it for restarts. Protect the TLS private-key file with operating-system permissions; it is separate from the encrypted infrastructure identity. Use a certificate trusted by the clients; the test's bundled self-signed key is solely a local fixture.
3. **Two-hop protocol (started):** `selectPrivateRouteV1` picks an ordered pair from fresh, independently signed descriptors bound to authenticated connection endpoints. It requires distinct relay identities and distinct observed IP domains, and refuses to select a route when that evidence is missing. This is a conservative network-diversity signal, not proof of independent operators. `createPrivateRequestV1` encrypts an operation for the destination and wraps its destination layer in an independently encrypted forwarding instruction for the entry. A local socket integration test now sends publication, search, and mailbox requests through two relay instances. The entry signs a short-lived forward to a destination in its verified directory; the destination handles the original v0.3 operation and encrypts its signed reply to the client's one-use response key. The entry refuses an unknown destination and never forwards a cleartext fallback. The test asserts that operation and reply identifiers are absent from entry-visible frames. The personal client uses this route in explicit experimental mode. A separate-process test confirms that destination sockets originate from the entry. DNS checks constrain the entry's claimed destination IP but do not prove independent operators or authenticate DNS. Cross-household validation and route rotation remain open.
4. **Connection authentication:** Long-lived relay links now require a fresh challenge from the receiving socket before the initiating relay signs its opening. The server rejects an opening captured from a different socket. Other request types already use short-lived signed requests or encrypted private envelopes; their signature and resource-limit audit remains open.
5. **Anonymous admission (started):** Privacy Pass Blind RSA blinding, issuance, scoped redemption, and a fsynced local spent-token log now work through a real publication request, including one manually issued token sent through the two-hop client. A configured relay can require these tokens, and an exact publication retry survives a restart. An experimental encrypted client wallet verifies manually issued tokens and durably reserves each one for an exact request and destination; a two-hop test redeems from that wallet. The [admission decision](v0.4-admission-decision.md) specifies volunteer issuance and fail-closed witness quorums. Desktop wallet integration, issuance policy, network-wide spent-token consensus, and partition tests remain open; the local log cannot stop a token being spent once at each of two partitioned relays.
6. **Traffic and fingerprint privacy (started):** Each private operation uses a short connection and a fresh request key; when multiple entry relays are configured, the client varies which one it tries first. The destination's encrypted request payload and encrypted signed reply now have authenticated random padding to 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, or 256 KiB. A 64-byte and a 4,096-byte operation produce equal-length entry-visible frames in a local test; crossing a bucket still reveals a size range. Connection timing, direction, request count, and bucket size remain visible. This changes the experimental private-envelope wire format; earlier v0.4 test nodes cannot decode the padded format.

   Each private client now queues work before opening discovery sockets or reserving admission tokens. Defaults collect waiting work for 250 ms, select up to four oldest requests, shuffle them, and delay each by a cryptographically random 0–250 ms. At most four requests are active at once, including discovery and destination fallbacks. The queue holds at most 64 outstanding requests and rejects work that waits more than five seconds to start. Limits are configurable through `RelayClientConfig.privateTraffic`. Each selected operation still uses its own route and connection; no combined multi-operation frame or cover traffic is added. This local scheduler does not mix different users' traffic.

   A fixed ten-second monotonic operation budget starts before scheduling and covers all entry/destination attempts, independent DNS, encryption, and reply processing; shorter stage limits and signed request expiry remain authoritative. Expiry cancels only that operation's queued work, native DNS queries, and active client sockets, with elapsed-time checks preventing late continuations from sending. `disconnect()` cancels all current operations; later calls can schedule fresh work. Neither action can undo an accepted operation. The exported `PrivateOperationError` distinguishes timeout/cancellation and `not-sent` versus `unknown` outcomes; discovery traffic may already have occurred even when the payload was not sent. Missing acknowledgements retain exact-operation wallet reservations. Relationship mailbox fanout uses one budget and preserves replies validated before expiry without claiming that every destination answered. Tests exercise stalled fallbacks, queue timing, DNS cancellation, fanout, and an accepted publication whose final reply is lost and whose exact retry succeeds with the same reserved token. See the [deadline behavior and limits](v0.4-quiet-period-decision.md#budgets-and-behavior), including event-loop limitations.

   The entry relay now shares a collection queue across private clients. Defaults hold the first waiting request for 750 ms, select up to 16 oldest requests when forwarding slots are available, and cryptographically shuffle that batch. Up to 16 forwards may run concurrently. The queue admits at most 64 outstanding requests and 8 MiB of serialized encrypted destination layers, counting queued and active forwards; these bounds do not cap total process memory or pre-decryption work. Waiting work expires after three seconds at most, and the entry requires more than one second of envelope lifetime remaining when dispatching. Client disconnect and relay shutdown remove waiting work and abort active forwards; cancellation cannot undo an operation already applied by the destination. Overload and expiry close the private request without bypassing the queue. The queue retains no durable payloads and adds no cover traffic. `RelayConfig.privateEntryMix` customizes the bounded limits; `false` explicitly disables request mixing. Omission enables the defaults for private entry requests.

   Encrypted replies use an independent queue at the entry with the same defaults: a 750 ms window, batches and concurrency capped at 16, at most 64 outstanding replies and 8 MiB of serialized encrypted replies, and a maximum three-second wait. The reply remains byte-for-byte unchanged and bound to its original client. Forwarding slots are released when the destination reply arrives; reply capacity remains charged until the client socket write callback completes. Both stages use the original request expiry. Cancellation and shutdown abort pending replies and stalled writes, and even the explicit unmixed return path bounds a stalled write by that expiry. Overflow never triggers immediate unbatched delivery. An operation may already have been accepted before a reply fails, so a missing reply is an unknown outcome, not evidence that the destination rejected the operation. Live socket tests preserve the accepted record through cancellation, shutdown, and reply overflow. `RelayConfig.privateReplyMix` configures the reply queue independently; omission enables its defaults, and `false` disables only reply mixing. The two queues together can retain up to 16 MiB of counted payloads; this is not a whole-process memory limit.

   The [eight-client timing diagnostic](../evals/private-timing-2026-09-29T13-48-27-722Z.md) completed 96 equal-size searches through real local sockets. In burst traffic, a timestamp-order attack matched actions to their first discovery send in 24/24 baseline cases and 4/24 scheduled cases. Entry-to-destination timing still matched 24/24 in both modes. Sparse traffic matched 24/24 at both observation points in both modes. A separate colluding-relay probe also joined every one-use request ID. The baseline is the minimum supported 10 ms window without jitter, not a scheduling bypass. All clients and relays share one process, event loop, and machine; these are diagnostic measurements, not Internet anonymity estimates. The scorer receives ground truth separately from the attacker, and incomplete traces invalidate a run.

   The [separate-process entry-mixing experiment](../evals/private-mix-2026-09-29T14-09-59-545Z.md) completed 192 searches, with eight logical clients and three trials for each mode/workload/network combination. Both modes use client scheduling; only the entry queue differs. Burst request-order matches fell from 20/24 to 2/24 on loopback and from 15/24 to 7/24 with simulated 20–60 ms application-frame delays. Sparse request matches remained 24/24 on loopback, and changed from 24/24 to 22/24 with delays. Reply-order matches were unchanged between modes: 24/24 on loopback, 10/24 for delayed bursts, and 24/24 for delayed sparse traffic. The lower delayed-burst reply score also occurred without mixing, so it is not evidence of reply protection from the queue. The median entry-to-destination interval increased by about 0.67–0.77 seconds across the four conditions. Full search completion p95 reached 4.8 seconds for delayed mixed bursts. The clients still share one process and all relays share a physical host; frame-delay simulation is not an Internet or packet-loss test.

   The [reply-mixing experiment](../evals/private-reply-2026-09-29T14-30-17-612Z.md) completed another 192 searches. Both modes retain request mixing; the treatment adds the independent reply queue. Loopback burst reply-order matches fell from 24/24 to 2/24, but delayed bursts changed from 5/24 to 6/24: this run shows no added benefit under simulated delays. Sparse reply matches stayed at 24/24 on loopback and increased from 20/24 to 24/24 with delays. These small rank-attack samples do not establish a reliable improvement or regression under noisy links. Median return latency increased by 0.73–0.77 seconds across the four conditions, and full completion p95 reached 5.63 seconds for delayed mixed bursts. Random forward-batch variation also changes forward scores; it is not evidence of a request-privacy benefit from reply batching. All clients still share one process and all relays share one host, destination, and size bucket. The older request-only command explicitly disables reply mixing in both modes to preserve that comparison.

   **Decision:** keep both queues experimental and observer protection open. The [quiet-period policy](v0.4-quiet-period-decision.md) selects zero added idle traffic/polling and no automatic quiet-period window increases. The single ten-second operation deadline and explicit encrypted publication hold are implemented. In the desktop private-transport pilot, Save on this device keeps the complete publication and its local item/key data encrypted, outside mailbox sync, until an explicit Send. Search/mailbox holds remain open. Holding prevents transmission, not correlation when an operation is eventually sent. The [offline cost analysis](../evals/quiet-period-budgets-2026-09-29.md) compares waiting and scheduled traffic without generating cover traffic or claiming measured energy use. Reply batching demonstrates a loopback burst benefit, while the simulated-delay and sparse results do not support a broader privacy claim. Production timing defaults still require stronger attacks and independent-path measurements. Clients cannot prove that a remote volunteer follows its batching policy. Neither mixing nor removing a shared request ID establishes protection when both relays collude: the entry knows the ciphertext it forwards.

   A prototype derives a public projection matrix per group and UTC month, but the live client still uses the static pilot matrix. In a deterministic 64-pair synthetic probe at the current 0.65 threshold, neighboring vectors matched 64/64 under both the static and scoped matrices; the exact same vector across groups or months matched 0/64. This is evidence that a simple epoch change would break matches at a boundary, not a real-text recall result. Query overlap, publication migration, and a real corpus evaluation must precede live rotation. Quiet-period policy implementation, automatic route diversity, and broader network-observer measurements remain open.

No v0.4 privacy claim is complete until the roadmap's end-to-end observation test passes.
