# Mailbox acknowledgements as prepaid maintenance

## Decision

An owner may acknowledge an envelope that a relay has already accepted without
spending another admission token. The request remains signed by the mailbox
owner and travels through the client's selected transport. Fetch, deposit,
publication writes and search still require admission when it is configured.

The distinction is storage admission: an ACK removes an existing envelope and
retains its deletion proof until the envelope expires. It cannot create a new
message, choose an arbitrary tombstone expiry, or create a tombstone for an
unknown ID through the client endpoint. Admission history availability should
not prevent a relay from completing this existing durability promise.

This changes the unreleased v0.4 protocol behavior. The
`mailbox-acknowledge` admission action remains in historical spend parsing and
verification; previously accepted spend records must still reopen and reject
conflicting reuse. A supplied capability does not purchase an additional ACK
allowance and is not spent by the ACK handler.

## Storage invariant

The relay already reserves **2,048 journal bytes per pending envelope** for its
future acknowledgement. Previously, one present ID plus 99 unknown IDs could
persist a signed request larger than that reserve. This increased the durability
commitment even with ample disk space; a global journal quota alone did not fix
the promise.

Before any journal write, the relay now compares the **complete serialized
record, including its wrapper and newline**, with 2,048 times the number of IDs
actually present. Exceeding that allowance returns
`acknowledgement_batch_too_large` without writing, deleting an envelope, or
running quota compaction. Zero present IDs return `acknowledged:0` without a
journal row. The global storage quota still applies independently.

The original signed request is retained intact. Trimming unknown IDs would
invalidate its signature and its use in replay and replication. The journal is
flushed before the in-memory deletion becomes visible. A successful retry cannot
increase the journal or resurrect a message.

Clients split validated requests of at most 100 unique IDs into signed batches
of at most 16. New owner requests that would persist a proof are also limited to
16 at the server; client behavior alone cannot guarantee that a raw sender
produces a proof portable to a replica with only one envelope left. This leaves room for the full proof even if only one ID in a batch
exists at that relay. Batches share the original operation deadline and existing
transport limits. A timeout can leave earlier batches acknowledged; retry is
safe. Success means every batch received a validated successful reply under the
existing destination/fallback rules. The returned receipt is the actual last
batch receipt, not a new aggregate signature.

### Existing large proofs

Historical parsing and replay still accept signed requests of up to 100 IDs.
Those signatures and stored rows are not rewritten. Repair may accept an old
large proof when enough present envelopes fund it, but it can still refuse one
when only a single envelope remains at the receiving replica. That pre-existing
case needs new small acknowledgements signed by the owner for the affected
replica. The fresh limit prevents creating new cases; it is not an automatic
migration of earlier proofs. Dropping the event or slicing its signed IDs would
not preserve the deletion evidence.

## Authentication and availability

The relay still checks the exact request schema, owner signature, timestamp and
transport rate limit. Publication ACKs must also match a stored publication's
key and mailbox. Relationship requests bind their mailbox to their relationship
key. A forged signature or changed mailbox cannot delete another owner's mail.

The client skips token reservation and policy observation only for ACKs. A
separate local check preserves the active wallet destination pins and the
desktop private-route requirement, including immediately before transmission.
It does not request a fresh policy, require tokens, or reject an otherwise
authorized ACK merely because the admission policy expired. A profile change
that removes a destination still prevents a pending ACK from going there.
Private route discovery, destination/key validation, encryption, mixing,
cancellation and the original deadlines still apply. ACK availability does not imply that new
work can proceed while policy or witness quorums are offline.

This does not remove network denial-of-service risks or make operation timing
indistinguishable. Signature verification and transport controls still consume
resources. Nor does it guarantee that every large mailbox can be cleared in one
interactive deadline. Independent relay availability and a reachable private
route remain necessary.

## Retention and evidence

Compaction must preserve the signed ACKs that actually remove envelopes in the
retained replay, in their original order. Merely retaining the latest ACK that
mentions an ID is insufficient: an earlier ACK may have released capacity, and
an ACK preceding that ID's deposit had no deletion effect. Selection also
considers future restarts after an earlier tombstone expires: a deposit that is
blocked today may need its following ACK then. Replicated deletion events must
preserve the strongest preceding barrier and the longest final live expiry. Repeated mentions of an
already-deleted long-lived ID must not retain every otherwise obsolete row.

Validation covers actual-byte quota refusal, unchanged files and store on
rejection, full signed proofs, retries, reopen, ownership rejection and ACKs
during admission unavailability. The real application latency evaluation is a
separate gate: this decision is not evidence that the 25 ms stream-delay
scenario or the whole v0.4 release is complete.
